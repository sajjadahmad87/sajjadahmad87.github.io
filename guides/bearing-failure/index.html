Mechanical Reliability

Common Causes of Bearing Failure in Industrial Equipment

Bearing failure is often blamed on the bearing itself, but the real cause frequently lies in lubrication, contamination, alignment, loading, installation or surrounding machine conditions. Correct diagnosis prevents repeated replacement without reliability improvement.

1. Lubrication problems

Too little lubricant allows metal-to-metal contact and temperature rise. Too much grease can also increase churning, heat and seal stress. Wrong lubricant type, incompatible grease or incorrect relubrication interval can shorten bearing life. Check the OEM requirement, operating temperature, speed and environmental condition rather than using one grease for every application.

2. Contamination

Dust, water, process chemicals and metal particles damage rolling surfaces and lubricant quality. Contamination may enter during relubrication, through failed seals or because the housing is exposed during maintenance. Clean handling practices and proper sealing are part of bearing reliability.

3. Misalignment and shaft condition

Angular or parallel misalignment can create uneven load across the bearing. Bent shafts, worn seats and incorrect fits can create similar symptoms. If bearings repeatedly fail on the same position, investigate the complete mechanical arrangement rather than treating the bearing as an isolated spare part.

4. Installation damage

Hammering through the rolling elements, applying force to the wrong race, poor heating practice or incorrect fitting tools can damage a new bearing before the machine starts. Installation procedures should control cleanliness, force, temperature and fit.

5. Overload and operating condition

Unexpected process load, belt over-tension, hydraulic or pneumatic forces and machine jams can exceed bearing design conditions. Compare actual duty with the original design. A larger bearing is not always the correct solution if the system is being overloaded.

6. Electrical damage

Motors controlled by variable-frequency drives may experience shaft-voltage or electrical-discharge problems under certain conditions. Fluting or pitting patterns can indicate electrical current passing through bearing surfaces. Correct grounding, insulated bearings or shaft-grounding arrangements may be required depending on the application.

Evidence matters: preserve failed bearings where practical. Photograph raceways, cages, seals, lubricant condition and mounting surfaces before disposal.

Early warning signs

  • Increasing vibration
  • Abnormal noise
  • Rising bearing temperature
  • Grease discoloration or leakage
  • Increasing motor current
  • Repeated coupling or seal problems

Prevent repeat failure

Combine condition monitoring with maintenance history. Trend vibration and temperature on critical assets, verify lubrication practice, inspect alignment after major work and record the exact failed position and symptoms. When repeat failures occur, use a structured RCA rather than simply shortening the replacement interval.
