Maintenance Planning

How to Build a Professional PPM Checklist

A strong planned preventive maintenance checklist guides a technician toward the failure mechanisms that matter. It should be specific enough to produce repeatable results, but practical enough to complete safely within the available maintenance window.

PPMChecklist designMaintenance planning

What you will be able to do

  • Convert an equipment failure mode into a clear maintenance task.
  • Choose whether the task belongs in running, stopped or isolated maintenance.
  • Define evidence and acceptance criteria without inventing generic limits.
  • Route defects into corrective work instead of hiding them inside a completed PPM.
  • Audit whether the checklist is improving reliability rather than only generating completed work orders.

Start with the asset and failure mode

Identify the exact asset, manufacturer, model, duty, operating environment and production consequence. A useful checklist is built around credible ways the asset can lose its required function. Review the OEM manual, breakdown history, repeat defects, technician observations, condition trends, FMEA results and approved site standards. A task with no connection to a failure mode, statutory requirement or performance need is a candidate for review.

Planning question: what deterioration should this task prevent, detect or quantify—and what decision will the recorded result support?

Write each task as a complete instruction

A field-ready task normally contains six parts:

  1. Action: inspect, clean, lubricate, measure, test, adjust or replace.
  2. Component: identify exactly what the technician must work on.
  3. Method: state the required instrument, observation or approved procedure.
  4. Condition: define whether the asset must be running, stopped or isolated.
  5. Acceptance criteria: reference the applicable OEM, design or approved site limit.
  6. Evidence: require the actual reading, condition code, defect note, photo reference or test result.

“Check motor” is not repeatable. A stronger instruction is: “With the motor operating under a comparable load, record phase currents using the approved method; compare phases and previous readings; escalate values outside approved equipment or site criteria.” It tells the technician what to do, what to record and how the result is used without inventing a universal limit.

Choose the maintenance state before scheduling

Separate tasks that can be completed while equipment is running from tasks requiring a controlled stop or full isolation. Grouping work by maintenance state reduces repeated shutdowns and prevents a checklist from encouraging unsafe shortcuts. State access requirements, permits, hazardous-energy controls, cooling or depressurization needs, guarding requirements and any competent-person restriction before the task instruction.

A PPM checklist does not authorize work. Approved site procedures, OEM instructions, permit-to-work (PTW) requirements, LOTO, risk assessments and competent-person controls take priority for electrical energy, pressure or steam systems, rotating equipment, chemicals, lifting, work at height, confined spaces and other hazards.

Set frequency from evidence

Start with mandatory inspection requirements and OEM recommendations. Then consider criticality, duty cycle, environment, utilization, failure history and the time between detectable deterioration and functional failure. Do not shorten every interval after a breakdown: first confirm that the existing task could detect or prevent the actual failure mode. Where deterioration is measurable, condition-based triggers may be more useful than adding another calendar task.

Document why the frequency was selected and who approved changes. Review the interval after modifications, duty changes, repeat failures, new condition data or significant findings. Site and OEM requirements always override general educational examples.

Arrange work in a safe, efficient sequence

  1. Confirm asset identity, scope, permits and maintenance state.
  2. Review open defects, recent breakdowns and previous abnormal readings.
  3. Complete external and running observations that are safe and authorized.
  4. Stop, isolate and verify zero-energy conditions where required.
  5. Clean before detailed inspection so defects are visible.
  6. Perform mechanical, lubrication, electrical and control checks in a logical route.
  7. Restore guards, tools and housekeeping before controlled return to service.
  8. Complete the authorized functional test and capture operating readings.
  9. Record defects, corrective work orders and any operating restriction.

Use consistent condition and escalation fields

A simple controlled vocabulary improves reporting. For example, use condition choices such as acceptable, monitor, corrective work required, or remove from service—defined by the organization. Never use a colour or priority label without a written meaning. The checklist should capture the defect, immediate risk control, required action, priority basis, responsible group and corrective work-order number.

Completing the PPM must not close unresolved findings. If storage, CMMS or connectivity fails, preserve the observation through the approved fallback process and enter it when the system is restored. Safety-critical failures require immediate escalation under site procedure.

Minimum fields for a professional checklist

  • Asset ID, description, location, manufacturer and model
  • Task ID, component and failure mode addressed
  • Maintenance state and hazard or isolation requirement
  • Task instruction, method or tool and acceptance criteria
  • Actual reading, unit and condition
  • Defect description, action, priority and corrective work order
  • Technician, completion date and supervisor verification where required
  • Photo or evidence reference without confidential or personal information

Audit checklist quality after use

Review a sample of completed PPMs and ask whether technicians recorded actual evidence, abnormal findings created corrective work, overdue defects were escalated and readings can be trended. Compare checklist findings with subsequent breakdowns. A task that repeatedly produces “OK” but never detects the failure it is intended to control needs redesign, a different method or a different maintenance strategy.

Useful improvement signals include repeat-failure rate, emergency work, PM-generated corrective actions, defect backlog age, schedule compliance, MTBF and downtime. Do not optimize for completion percentage alone: a quickly closed checklist with no meaningful evidence can hide risk rather than control it.

Common checklist design failures

  • Copying a generic checklist across different models and duties.
  • Using vague verbs without a method or acceptance criterion.
  • Combining running checks and isolation work without stating the maintenance state.
  • Entering “checked OK” instead of actual readings or observed condition.
  • Embedding major repairs inside a short inspection window.
  • Using invented limits instead of approved OEM, design or site criteria.
  • Closing the parent PPM while corrective findings have no traceable work order.
  • Keeping obsolete tasks after equipment or operating conditions change.
